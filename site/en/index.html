What each translation will include

All books

The 66 books and the deuterocanonical books, labelled as such. Each tradition can read in its own order.

Original languages

Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek alongside the text, word by word, with lemma and morphology.

Manuscripts and variants

Where manuscripts differ, the variant, the witnesses and what changes in meaning are documented.

Strong's numbers

Every word linked to the original, so you can study a term inside the translation itself.

Related verses

Cross-references and Old Testament quotations in the New, with the form the author quoted.

Section headings and introductions

Original section headings and an introduction to each book, without taking a confessional side.

Notes and commentary

Short notes in the text. Commentary in a separate layer, with author and tradition in view.

Maps and illustrations

Only freely licensed material or our own work, with the origin recorded.

  • Read, copy, print, distribute

  • Sell it or charge for a service

You must

  • Name the exact version: bibleme + version + language + release (for digital use, the commit)
  • Include the project link and the license

Not included

  • Using the "bibleme" mark to suggest your modified version is official
  • Warranties: the text is provided "as is"

How we translate

From the originals, with double review.

  1. From the originals

    We start from freely licensed editions of the Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek, not from another translation.

  2. Independent draft

    The translator writes in their own words and records the reasoning.

  3. Comparison

    Only afterwards do we compare with well-known translations, such as the Jerusalem Bible, the NIV-family translations and the Brazilian ARA, to find errors and gauge clarity. Never as a source of text.

  4. Double review

    A reviewer of the originals and a reviewer of the language approve each passage.

  5. Read aloud

    Two people read it aloud, and rhythm and clarity get tuned.

  6. Stable release

    Frozen, citable versions, while the living text keeps evolving.

Why don't we copy? Translations, notes and section headings have owners. Respecting that protects the project and everyone who uses it. We plan to have an intellectual property lawyer review the policy before the first stable release.

Roadmap

One step at a time, with deliveries along the way.

A whole Bible takes years. So we release in coherent blocks, with stable versions, instead of waiting for the end.

  1. 0. FoundationsWe are here. Decide license, sources, philosophy, format, process, name and distribution in the open proposals.
  2. 1. PilotRuth, Jonah and Philemon (narrative, poetry and letter) to test the whole cycle.
  3. 2. First blocksThe Gospels and Psalms, with the first stable release.
  4. 3. CoverageThe rest of the New and Old Testaments and the deuterocanonical books.
  5. 4. More languagesNew teams, with the same process and the same quality bar.

FAQ

What people usually ask.

Is it free? Can I sell it?

Both. CC BY 4.0 allows free and commercial use. You only need to credit the version, link to the project and say so if you changed the text.

Do you copy the NIV, the ARA or the Jerusalem Bible?

No. We translate from the original texts. Those Bibles serve as a point of comparison for fidelity and clarity, but nothing from them enters the project: no verses, no notes, no headings. For the AI draft of the New Testament, no published translation was used as a base or as support.

Who reviews the text?

Each passage goes through a reviewer of the originals, a reviewer of the language and a read-aloud pass. Everything is recorded and open to comments. That is the planned process: the AI draft of the New Testament has so far only been cross-reviewed by two AIs, and no person has reviewed it.

What is the project's theological tradition?

The text aims to say what the original says. Interpretation lives in a separate layer, labelled by author and tradition. Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and people of no religion are welcome, and the deuterocanonical books are included.

Can I help without knowing Hebrew or Greek?

Of course. There is plenty to do: reviewing language, reading aloud, drawing maps, writing studies, translating the documentation, coding. Start by commenting on the proposals.

Can I translate into my language?

Yes, that is the goal. Open the "New language" issue and tell us how you would do it. Each language has its own team, and someone who can check against the originals is essential.

What about artificial intelligence?

AI is support, not an author: whoever submits a contribution answers for it and declares whether AI helped and how (RFC-0004, in Portuguese). The text is also published in AI-ready formats, with attribution.

The caution has a reason: models can reproduce protected translations from memory. So an AI draft of the biblical text does not count as an independent draft: it is risk material and only counts after a similarity check and human review.

Where AI has already been used. The founding documents (README, license guide, policies, source register and proposals) were drafted with AI support (Claude, by Anthropic) and reviewed by the founder. There is also an AI-produced draft of the New Testament (Claude and Codex, by OpenAI), translated from the Greek (SBLGNT) and published as an experiment in quarantine (in Portuguese): no human review, no standing as approved text, and a notice on every page.

How does this relate to the bibleme app?

bibleme is one possible user of the text. The translation project has independent governance: the app receives the text on the same terms as anyone else.
